Conversational Privacy Patterns

Home About Publications Datasets & Tools Talks Teaching Art X Science

A catalog of nine patterns for communicating privacy information clearly, precisely and comprehensibly in conversational interfaces, such as chatbots and voice assistants. Existing privacy patterns assume graphical interfaces; these patterns address privacy question answering (Privacy QA) in dialogue. This page presents the catalog from the paper below in a browsable wiki format. It is intended for developers, compliance professionals, privacy engineers, UX and conversation designers, and governance teams.

Overview

The catalog follows the pattern format of Coplien and Schmidt [7] (context, problem, forces, solution, resulting context, known uses, examples, related patterns) and builds on the privacy patterns collection of Colesky et al. [6] and the conversational design pattern libraries of Moore [37] and OpenDialog [44]. It follows the hierarchical approach of Lobato et al. [34]: one higher-level pattern, Answer Privacy Questions, expresses the basic need of allowing conversations about privacy. Lower-level patterns solve its subproblems and fall into two categories.

  • Dialogue Management
    Patterns that manage the interaction: ambiguous or unanswerable questions, layered responses, proactive help. These are driven by conversational forces and user actions.
  • Word Choice
    Patterns that shape how privacy information is phrased: layman’s terms, active voice, precision, no overpromising. These are driven by the user’s need for comprehensible and precise communication.

The two categories complement each other. A system that manages dialogue well but generates vague or overly technical responses still fails to inform users, and well-phrased outputs help little if the system misunderstood the user’s intent.

The paper counts nine patterns: the root pattern and eight lower-level patterns. Detect Ambiguous Questions and Clarify Ambiguous Questions are sub-patterns that realize Resolve Ambiguous Questions, so eleven entries are listed here. Patterns can be implemented in the front end (e.g., interaction design) or within the conversational agent itself (e.g., word choice mechanisms), and no pattern is strictly tied to one layer. The catalog is not exhaustive. It is a starting point for collecting further Conversational Privacy Patterns.

The patterns

Answer Privacy Questions #

Root pattern · refined by all patterns below

Context
Conversational AI systems are used to support users in their daily tasks. Thereby, they can collect or request personal information in the form of text or voice data. To comply with legal requirements and inform users about how their data is processed, data controllers commonly provide privacy notices.
Problem
Privacy notices are often long, complex and not readily accessible in CAI interfaces, making it difficult for data controllers to sufficiently inform users.
Forces
  • Users need to be informed clearly, understandably and without requiring additional effort about data processing practices when using Conversational AI systems.
  • Data protection and privacy regulations require users to be transparently informed about data collection and processing, but full privacy notices are often too complex and lengthy to present in conversational AI interfaces.
Solution
Answer privacy questions by allowing users to request information about data processing practices in natural language, depending on the modality of the system. Specifically, privacy question-answering can be implemented by data controllers.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, it should be possible for users to request information about data processing practices in natural language. This helps to inform users about personal data handling and fosters understandability and transparency. However, it raises new challenges, such as handling ambiguous questions and responding with an appropriate level of detail, which are addressed by the follow-up patterns of this catalog.
Known uses
Conversational Privacy Bots proposed by Harkous et al. [20], Generative AI-based Privacy Assistants by Hamid et al. [19] and LLM-Powered Interactive Privacy Policy Assessment by Freiberger et al. [11]. However, they do not explicitly implement all lower-level patterns described in this catalog. For instance, Harkous et al. [20] describe a system architecture that combines a retrieval module connected to a knowledge base, a confidence threshold for identifying unanswerable questions to trigger either an apology or a link to the full policy, and a mechanism for collecting user feedback. In contrast, more recent LLM-based implementations primarily rely on prompting techniques to generate answers to privacy questions.
Related patterns
This pattern complements privacy transparency patterns [54] and privacy patterns that fall under the strategy of informing data subjects [6]. Within this catalog, Answer Privacy Questions is structured by Resolve Unanswerable Questions, Resolve Ambiguous Questions, Layered Privacy Answers, and Proactive Assistance, and expressed through In Layman’s Terms, Promise Pitfall, Embrace Active Voice, and Be Precise.

Dialogue Management patterns

Layered Privacy Answers #

Dialogue Management

Context
Due to compliance with legal requirements, privacy notices tend to be long, complex and difficult to understand. Therefore, the extraction of answers to privacy-related questions can result in long answers which are not suitable for Conversational AI systems.
Problem
When providing answers to privacy-related questions, the controller needs to balance detailedness and understandability. Thereby, the level of detail and length need to match the modality of the Conversational AI system, e.g., text or voice-based.
Forces
  • Detailed privacy information is necessary to ensure transparency, but can overwhelm users if too lengthy or complex.
  • Different modalities (e.g., voice vs. text) have varying capacities for presenting information.
  • Users have different information needs, requiring the system to adapt the amount of information to provide.
Solution
Provide layered privacy answers by structuring responses to privacy-related questions into multiple levels of detail, starting with a concise, high-level answer and allowing users to request additional information incrementally. Make use of Extended Telling conversational patterns (OpenDialog) to receive cues on whether users would like to have additional information or whether the provided information is sufficient. This approach is closely related to the principle of progressive disclosure in user interface design [24], which has been adopted in the design of transparent intelligent systems [56, 65].
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, users receive information tailored to their needs, which supports transparency and choice. The system highlights crucial aspects first while allowing deeper exploration of privacy details on demand. However, new design challenges arise in determining which information should be prioritized in the initial layer to ensure legal compliance if users do not request further details, and how to balance layered privacy answers with regulatory transparency requirements.
Known uses
While elements of layered information presentation appear in related domains, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are no documented implementations that explicitly apply Layered Privacy Answers to Privacy QA scenarios.
Examples
Question
How can you guarantee my location information is secure?
Bad example
List all measures that ensure that information is secure.
Good example
“We design our systems with your security and privacy in mind and maintain physical, electronic and procedural safeguards to protect your information. Would you like to know more about our safeguards?”
Related patterns
This pattern implements Answer Privacy Questions by providing a concrete strategy for structuring answers to privacy-related queries in conversational interfaces. In addition, it complements external privacy patterns such as Layered Policy Design, by transferring the idea of layered disclosure from static privacy policies to interactive Privacy QA.

Resolve Ambiguous Questions #

Dialogue Management

Context
Ambiguous questions are common in privacy dialogues, due to the complexity of the privacy terminology, varying levels of privacy knowledge, in addition to the user being uncertain about the capabilities of the system in responding to privacy questions. Ambiguous questions refer to situations where user queries allow multiple plausible interpretations or lack context, which makes it impossible to determine the user’s intent with sufficient confidence.
Problem
Ambiguity in user questions can lead to misunderstandings between the system and the user, resulting in confusion, inaccurate responses, or incorrect actions. Despite advances in NLP, ambiguity remains difficult to handle reliably in conversational systems [32, 53].
Forces
  • Users can ask ambiguous or underspecified questions, increasing the risk of misunderstandings or incorrect system actions.
  • Ambiguity must be addressed to avoid incorrect or misleading answers, but repeatedly interrupting users for clarification can harm the conversational experience.
  • Privacy-related answers must remain accurate and compliant, even when user intent is unclear.
Solution
Resolve ambiguity by first identifying when a privacy-related question is ambiguous and then applying appropriate conversational strategies to clarify the user’s intended meaning before providing an answer.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, the system can address and react to ambiguous questions, which improves response accuracy and enhances user experience by reducing confusion and ensuring accurate privacy information.
Known uses
Adjacent areas, such as information retrieval [58], are actively researching how to resolve ambiguities. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are currently no documented uses of these strategies specifically in the context of Privacy QA.
Related patterns
This pattern is realized by Detect Ambiguous Questions and Clarify Ambiguous Questions, and structures privacy-related dialogue within Answer Privacy Questions.

Detect Ambiguous Questions #

Dialogue Management

Context
Users ask privacy-related questions using natural language, which may be underspecified or context-dependent.
Problem
If ambiguity is not detected, the system may provide an incorrect or misleading answer, potentially resulting in privacy risks or loss of user trust.
Forces
  • Overly sensitive ambiguity detection may falsely flag clear questions, leading to unnecessary clarification.
  • Insufficient ambiguity detection may cause the system to answer prematurely, increasing the risk of incorrect or misleading privacy information.
Solution
Detect ambiguous questions by identifying signals such as multiple plausible interpretations, vague references, or missing parameters using NLP techniques (e.g., ambiguity detection models, uncertainty estimation, or confidence thresholds).
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, the system can reliably identify when a privacy-related question cannot be safely answered without additional clarification.
Known uses
Prior research has explored ambiguity detection in natural language and question answering systems [53]. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, these approaches have not been explicitly applied or evaluated in the context of Privacy QA.
Examples
Question
Can you delete my data?
Detected ambiguity
It is unclear which data is being referred to, which likely affects the possibility of deletion.
Related patterns
This pattern supports Resolve Ambiguous Questions and depends on Clarify Ambiguous Questions to fully resolve ambiguity.

Clarify Ambiguous Questions #

Dialogue Management

Context
A privacy-related question has been identified as ambiguous and cannot be answered reliably without further information.
Problem
By responding to ambiguous questions, the system risks providing incorrect or misleading privacy information.
Forces
  • Clarification is necessary to resolve ambiguity, but excessive or poorly phrased clarification requests can frustrate users and disrupt the conversational flow.
  • Clarification prompts must convey sufficient information to disambiguate the question, but they should remain concise and understandable, avoiding legal or technical overload.
Solution
Clarify ambiguous questions by explicitly engaging the user in a clarification step before providing a privacy-related answer. The system should generate targeted clarification prompts that expose alternative interpretations, request missing information, or rephrase the original question for confirmation.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, the system can disambiguate user intent through conversational interaction and provide accurate, context-appropriate privacy information.
Known uses
Clarification strategies have been studied in adjacent research areas such as information retrieval and dialogue systems, where systems generate clarification questions to confirm the user’s intended reference [18, 64]. With the emergence of large language models, clarification generation has been explored using prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thought reasoning [58, 61]. Other approaches include generating paraphrases of ambiguous questions to surface multiple interpretations and allow users to confirm the intended meaning (e.g., [42]). However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, these approaches have not been explicitly applied or evaluated in the context of Privacy QA.
Examples
Syntactic ambiguity — question
Can I delete data collected from my activity?
Bad practice
“Yes, you can manage your data in the settings.”
Good practice
“Do you mean data collected directly from your activity, or data about your activity such as activity logs?”
Semantic ambiguity — question
How do you use my profile information?
Bad practice
“We use your profile information to enhance our services.”
Good practice
“When you say ‘profile information’, do you mean account details such as your name and contact information, or inferred information such as interests?”
Related patterns
This pattern realizes Resolve Ambiguous Questions and depends on Detect Ambiguous Questions and complements conversational patterns such as Repair. It may be further supported by Layered Privacy Answers and Proactive Assistance.

Resolve Unanswerable Questions #

Dialogue Management

Context
The ability to answer privacy-related questions is often limited to the information available in the privacy notice. As a result, users may encounter situations where their privacy-related questions cannot be answered based on the policy, either because the information is missing, the scenario is atypical, or the question is vague or ill-formed (Ravichander et al. [48]).
Problem
Data controllers need to detect and handle unanswerable questions. Unanswerability refers to the fact that a question cannot be answered using the privacy notice due to missing information, policy silence, atypical scenarios, or ill-formed or ambiguous questions.
Forces
  • Privacy notices may provide general guidelines, but they may lack explicit details, comprehensive explanations or information on emerging scenarios, particularly for users with domain-specific backgrounds.
  • Users may ask privacy-related questions, but these questions may fall outside the scope of the privacy notice.
  • Providing fallback responses can ensure the system always replies, but generic answers may frustrate users.
Solution
Resolve unanswerable questions by using techniques such as LLM prompting [55] for detection and differentiate between types of unanswerability (e.g., technical limitations, missing policy information, or vague questions). To handle unanswerable questions, predefined responses may sometimes be necessary to satisfy legal or compliance requirements, but they should be complemented with more flexible fallback strategies. Here, conversational design patterns such as Repair and Transfer [41, 44] can guide the system in overcoming unanswerability. Additionally, self-alignment approaches can be used to detect unanswerability and offer explanations to the user [8].
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, the system can resolve unanswerable questions effectively, ensuring trustworthiness of the system and avoiding user frustration if questions cannot be answered based on the information provided.
Known uses
So far, existing approaches to handling unanswerable questions rely on general-purpose datasets, such as QnotA [1], which are not specific to the Privacy QA domain.
Examples
Question
Does the voice assistant perform emotion recognition on my recordings?
Bad practice
“Yes, all recordings are analyzed.”
Good practice
“There is no information on performing emotion recognition for your recordings in the privacy notice. For more information, please contact support.”
Related patterns
Resolve Unanswerable Questions depends on Resolve Ambiguous Questions to handle questions that are inherently ambiguous and cannot be reliably answered. It is supported by Word Choice patterns such as Be Precise or Promise Pitfall, which improve the clarity of responses without setting unrealistic expectations. Additionally, it complements external conversational design patterns such as Repair and Transfer [41, 44], which offer methods for ensuring smooth progression of a conversation.

Proactive Assistance #

Dialogue Management

Context
Certain legal regulations give users the right to exert control over their personal information when shared with the data controller. More generally, users expect that they can easily configure privacy preferences and settings and have an influence on what is stored and processed by the data controller when interacting with a Conversational AI system.
Problem
Users need to be able to adjust their privacy preferences and exercise control over their personal information within a conversational interface.
Forces
  • Users expect to control their personal data, but may be unaware of which privacy controls exist and how to access them through natural language.
  • Users like to change privacy preferences without switching modalities.
Solution
Provide proactive assistance to users by giving guidance on how to adjust privacy preferences and exert control, such as deleting or exporting personal information, using natural language interactions. Conversational patterns such as Instructions and Offer of Help [37] can support the development of proactive conversations.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, users can easily exert control over their data and become more informed about possible privacy settings. However, because control actions are handled through natural language, misunderstandings and incorrect preference changes can occur. Other patterns, such as In Layman’s Terms or avoiding ambiguity, can be used to mitigate these risks.
Known uses
First approaches towards proactive agents have been explored [35, 66], however, these are more general in scope or focus on specific domains such as travel planning. They have yet to be applied in the context of Privacy QA.
Examples
Question
Can I delete all my data?
Bad practice
“You can delete your data in the privacy settings.”
Good practice
“You can delete your data in your privacy settings. Would you like me to guide you through the process now?”
Related patterns
Within this catalog, Proactive Assistance depends on Resolve Ambiguous Questions to ensure that guidance does not inadvertently set incorrect preferences, and is supported by Word Choice patterns such as Be Precise, Promise Pitfall, and In Layman’s Terms, which improve the clarity and comprehensibility of proactive messages. This pattern relates to privacy patterns that fall into the category of Control [21].

Word Choice patterns

In Layman’s Terms #

Word Choice

Context
Data controllers are required to provide clear explanations and ensure that users fully understand data processing practices. Important information should not be overlooked or overheard by the users.
Problem
Users need to easily understand and comprehend privacy-related information provided through conversational interfaces.
Forces
  • Privacy notices often contain complex legal and technical terminology that users find difficult to understand.
  • Voice-only conversational interfaces limit the amount of information that can be presented at once, increasing the risk that users miss important details.
  • Simplifying or summarising privacy information can improve comprehension, but oversimplification risks losing legally relevant meaning or introducing inaccuracies.
  • Users may have different levels of familiarity with the terminology, requiring flexible ways to clarify or repeat information.
Solution
Use layman’s terms to convey privacy-related information using clear, non-technical language, short sentences, and active voice, while preserving legal accuracy through alignment with approved policy wording. Conversational design patterns on repair can be integrated to allow users to request definitions of unknown terms, to request repetitions or check audibility.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, users will be more likely to understand the privacy information provided. When unfamiliar terms come up, the system can explain them in simple language. However, to support this, the system needs to include extra knowledge or definitions of uncommon terms and be designed to handle repair strategies. The effectiveness of this pattern may be evaluated through user studies measuring comprehension.
Known uses
Privacy-aware wording can, for instance, be found in “Nutrition Labels” for privacy [22]. Moreover, a recent study has explored the use of LLMs to simplify legal language [60], yet not specifically with a focus on privacy notices.
Examples
Question
Can you explain your data retention policy?
Bad practice
“Our data retention policy stipulates that we may retain certain personal information for a period of time that is necessary to fulfil the purposes for which it was collected or as required by applicable laws or regulations.”
Good practice
“We only store and retain data necessary to perform our functions, such as processing voice commands, answering questions, and providing personalized recommendations.” (Provide a link to the privacy notice only in text-based conversational interfaces.)
Related patterns
This pattern is supported by Word Choice patterns such as Embrace Active Voice and Be Precise. Additionally, it interacts with Promise Pitfall to avoid overpromising or misleading simplifications. It also complements the external privacy pattern on Privacy Aware Wording.

Promise Pitfall #

Word Choice

Context
Users of Conversational AI systems may inquire about the possibility of receiving guarantees or assurances concerning the privacy and security of their personal data.
Problem
Privacy is a complex and multifaceted concept, and guaranteeing absolute privacy in all circumstances may be practically impossible. There are inherent risks and uncertainties associated with data handling, storage, and transmission, which may never be completely eliminated. Making absolute claims can create unrealistic expectations and potentially mislead users about the level of privacy protection provided.
Forces
  • Absolute privacy guarantees are often unfeasible, but users may not fully understand the technical limitations of privacy protections and may expect unconditional guarantees.
  • Overstating privacy protections can mislead users.
Solution
Avoid Promise Pitfall by setting realistic expectations and avoiding absolute or overpromising claims about privacy protection. This may include prefacing answers with disclaimers about limitations and factual descriptions of data practices rather than speculative assurances.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, users have more realistic expectations about the system’s ability to provide complete and infallible privacy protection. They understand limitations and complexities involved in ensuring privacy.
Known uses
Techniques for reducing the overconfidence of LLMs, such as expressing uncertainty through “linguistic confidence”, have been explored in general dialogue settings [33, 36], but have not yet been applied specifically to Privacy QA scenarios.
Examples
Question
Can you guarantee that my personal information will never be shared with third parties?
Bad practice
“Yes, rest assured that we never share your personal information with a third party.”
Good practice
“We cannot guarantee that your data will never be shared with third parties, as legal obligations may require us to share information with authorised entities. However, we strictly adhere to privacy laws and continuously evaluate our policies to protect your personal information.”
Related patterns
This pattern is supported by internal patterns such as In Layman’s Terms and Be Precise, which improve clarity, and interacts with dialogue management patterns such as Resolve Ambiguous Questions and Resolve Unanswerable Questions to ensure potential misunderstandings are resolved before promise pitfalls. In addition, this pattern complements the privacy pattern on Privacy Aware Wording.

Embrace Active Voice #

Word Choice

Context
Privacy notices may be written in passive voice to reduce legal liability.
Problem
Users may be unable to determine who is accountable for privacy practices when information is conveyed in passive or vague language.
Forces
  • Communication must be legally accurate, but legal phrasing in passive voice can obscure responsibility and increase the risk of misinterpretation.
  • Transparency requires stating specific responsibilities, but privacy notices often avoid specifics to reduce legal liability, leading to vague explanations.
Solution
Embrace active voice to ensure that the provider takes ownership of the system’s actions and explicitly states who is responsible for privacy measures, data handling and privacy protection.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, the system responses use active voice, which provides clear and straightforward communication by explicitly stating the subject performing the action. Users will better understand the system’s actions, intentions and responsibilities for protecting user data.
Known uses
The use of active voice to promote clarity and accountability is widely adopted in official communication guidelines. For example, the VA.gov Design System explicitly recommends active voice in all user-facing content. Similarly, in Europe, plain language initiatives such as the European Union’s guidelines on “Accessible Publishing” promote active voice. While these guidelines are not specific to conversational AI or Privacy QA, they demonstrate established best practices for using active voice in communication.
Examples
Question
What personal information do you collect on me?
Bad practice
“Personal information is collected to provide you with our services. The information that is collected includes your name, email address, and phone number.”
Good practice
“We collect your name, email address, and phone number to provide you with our services.”
Related patterns
This pattern refines Answer Privacy Questions by improving the clarity of responses. It is closely related to word choice patterns such as In Layman’s Terms and Be Precise, which also aim to improve understandability and reduce ambiguity. This pattern complements the privacy pattern on Privacy Aware Wording.

Be Precise #

Word Choice

Context
Users may ask questions about specific types of information or data practices.
Problem
Users may receive vague or abstract answers that address privacy questions only in general terms without clearly specifying how their personal data is handled. Abstract answers are those that do not explicitly identify the type of data, action, or scenario the user asked about. This can lead to confusion, misinterpretation and reduced trust in the system, as users may perceive the lack of clarity as a lack of transparency or reliability in handling their privacy concerns. While answers may be legally accurate, they may be overly generic and imprecise.
Forces
  • Abstract or vague responses make it difficult for users to understand how their data is handled, reducing transparency.
  • Privacy notices may be written to avoid specifics and reduce legal liability, which can result in imprecise answers.
Solution
Be precise by formulating responses that explicitly state the specific data type or action referenced by the user and by avoiding unnecessary generalizations. This can include breaking down multi-part questions, clearly stating the scope of the answer, and incorporating contextual information to tailor responses.
Resulting context
After applying this pattern, the system provides clear and specific responses to privacy-related questions, which improves user comprehension and trust. This precision reduces misinterpretation, supports informed decision-making, and helps facilitate compliance with privacy regulations.
Known uses
Related known uses cover vagueness detection and analysis of privacy notices [27, 57]. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are no known uses that specifically implement precision and resolve vagueness in Privacy QA.
Examples
Question
Are you sharing my name?
Bad practice
“We do not share your personal information.”
Good practice
“We do not share your name.”
Related patterns
This pattern complements In Layman’s Terms to ensure that precise responses remain understandable and with Embrace Active Voice to clarify responsibility. Additionally, it also complements the privacy pattern on Privacy Aware Wording.

Using and evaluating the patterns

Evaluation. The paper proposes evaluation at three levels. (1) Are the patterns correctly implemented in the system? This is hard with non-deterministic LLMs; automated test cases, code audits and prompt refinement can help. (2) Does the observable behaviour reflect the intended effect of each pattern? Methods include expert judgment (e.g., by legal professionals), linguistic analysis and LLM-as-a-judge metrics. (3) Do the patterns improve comprehensibility and usability for users? This calls for user studies, comprehension tasks or surveys.

Applications. The paper suggests using the patterns for prompt engineering (e.g., zero-shot and few-shot prompts and prompt patterns such as “The refusal breaker” [63] combined with Resolve Unanswerable Questions); for evaluating Privacy QA beyond general readability, including annotating answers for precision and non-legal language and classifying questions by ambiguity and unanswerability; for instruction tuning and synthetic data generation; and for detecting vague or non-compliant language in privacy notices, which may also benefit data protection authorities and businesses.
